# Lesson: When Health Changes Your Sex Life
hero: guide-thinking
reflection: What has a health change taken from your intimate life, and what might still be possible?
takeaway: Health changes call for adapting intimacy, not giving it up.

## You Are Not Broken
art: guide-encourage
plain: Many people with long-term conditions have satisfying sex lives, often in new ways.

Chronic pain, fatigue and long-term conditions such as arthritis, diabetes, heart disease, endometriosis, MS or long COVID can change what sex feels like and how often you want it. This is common, and it does not mean intimacy is over.

Many people with long-term conditions have satisfying intimate lives, often by changing what intimacy looks like. (See Disability and Sexuality for more.)

## Grief Is Normal
art: guide-meditate
plain: It is OK to miss what sex used to be like before you build something new.

It is natural to miss how things used to be, for you and for a partner. Naming that loss, alone or together, often makes it easier to try something new.

check: Since her diagnosis, Lee feels sad that sex isn't what it was. What does this lesson suggest?
- She should stop thinking about sex
- *Missing how things were is normal, and naming it can help her adapt
- She should hide it from her partner
> Grief over what has changed is natural. Naming it is often the first step to finding new ways to be close.

## Look Beyond One Kind of Sex
art: guide-thinking-c
plain: Intimacy can include touch, massage, oral sex, toys, kissing and closeness.

If one kind of sex is painful or tiring, intimacy can include many other things: kissing, massage, mutual touch, oral sex, toys, bathing together or simply lying close. (See Pleasure & Satisfaction for redefining pleasure.)

# Lesson: Planning Around Energy and Pain
hero: guide-practice-dumbbell
reflection: When in your day or week do you have the most energy and the least pain?
takeaway: Plan around your good times, set yourself up for comfort, and pace yourself.

## Budget Your Energy
art: guide-daily-spin
plain: Plan intimacy for times you have energy, not the end of a draining day.

Many people with chronic illness think of energy as a limited daily budget. If sex matters to you, plan it for a time you usually have energy, not after a day that has already used it up.

## Time It With Your Treatment
art: guide-lesson-reading
plain: Some people feel best at certain times after medicine or treatment.

Some people find there is a time of day, or a time after taking medicine, when pain is lowest. Notice your patterns and plan around them. Ask your clinician before changing when you take any medicine.

check: Ravi's pain is lowest mid-morning. How could he use that?
- Change his medicine timing without telling his doctor
- Only have sex late at night when he's exhausted
- *Plan intimate time for mornings when he feels better
> Planning around the times you feel best is a simple, effective adjustment. Talk to a clinician before changing medicine timing.

## Set Up for Comfort
art: guide-meditate-b
plain: Pillows, wedges, warmth and positions that need less effort can make a big difference.

- Use pillows or wedges to support joints and take strain off your back.
- Try positions that need less effort, such as lying side by side.
- Warm up first: a warm bath or shower can ease stiff muscles.
- Use lubricant generously.
- Agree on a simple word or signal to pause or change position.

## Pace Yourself
art: guide-thinking-b
plain: Shorter sessions and breaks are fine. Stop before pain builds.

Shorter sessions, breaks and stopping before pain builds are all fine. Intimacy does not need to follow a script or end in a particular way.

# Lesson: Partner, Not Just Caregiver
hero: guide-high-five
reflection: Which parts of your relationship feel like care, and which feel like romance?
takeaway: Keep caregiving and partnership separate, and look after both people.

## When Care Crowds Out Romance
art: guide-study-together
plain: Helping with medical care can make it hard to see each other as lovers.

When one partner helps with medical care, like medicines, bathing or appointments, it can become hard for both of you to feel like romantic partners. The person receiving care may feel like a patient. The person giving care may feel like a nurse.

## Keep Some Time Just for You Two
art: guide-high-five
plain: Set aside time that is about connection, not care tasks.

Try to keep some time that is only about your relationship, with no care tasks in it. Where possible, outside help with some care, from family, friends or services, can give you room to be partners again.

check: Kim helps her partner with daily care and no longer feels romantic. What might help?
- Doing even more care tasks to show love
- *Setting aside time for connection that has no care tasks in it
- Avoiding the subject altogether
> Separating care time from partner time helps both people feel like partners again.

## Look After the Caregiver Too
art: guide-meditate-c
plain: Caregivers burn out. Their rest and support matter too.

Caregivers often put their own needs last and can burn out. Rest, friends, support groups and their own health care matter. A caregiver who is looked after can show up better for the relationship.

## Many Partners, Shared Care
art: guide-story-chat
plain: In larger families or networks, care and intimacy can be shared out openly.

In relationships with more than two people, care and intimacy can be shared across the network. Talk openly about who does what, so no one is overloaded and no one feels left out.

# Lesson: Talking With Your Clinicians
hero: guide-story-chat-c
reflection: What is one question about sex you have never asked a clinician, and how could you ask it?
takeaway: Sex is a health topic. Ask, and keep asking until you get help.

## Sex Is a Health Topic
art: guide-lesson-reading-b
plain: Your sexual health deserves the same care as any other part of your health.

Clinicians do not always ask about sex, so you may need to bring it up. Your sexual health is part of your health, and you deserve help with it.

## Ask About Side Effects
art: guide-thinking
plain: Many medicines affect desire, arousal or orgasm. There are often alternatives.

Many common medicines, including some for depression, blood pressure and pain, can affect desire, arousal or orgasm. If you notice changes, tell your clinician. There may be other options.

**Do not stop or change a medicine on your own.** Some medicines are dangerous to stop suddenly. (See Common Sexual Concerns for more on medicines and sexual function.)

check: Jordan's new medicine has lowered his desire. What should he do?
- Stop taking it right away
- *Tell his clinician and ask about other options
- Accept it and say nothing
> Stopping some medicines suddenly is dangerous. Talking to a clinician opens up safe options.

## Bring Questions
art: guide-study-together
plain: Writing questions down makes awkward topics easier to raise.

Writing questions down before an appointment makes them easier to ask. For example:

- "Could my medicine be affecting my sex drive?"
- "Is it safe for me to have sex with my condition?"
- "Can you refer me to a pelvic floor physical therapist or a sex therapist?"

## Keep Asking
art: guide-encourage-c
plain: If one clinician brushes you off, it is fine to ask someone else.

If a clinician dismisses your concerns, you can ask again, ask for a referral, or see someone else. (See Common Sexual Concerns for finding a provider.)
