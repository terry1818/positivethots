## 1. The Pause
**Module:** `consent-fundamentals`
**Story slug:** `the-pause`
**Difficulty:** 1 · **XP reward:** 50

### Characters
- **Maya (she/her)** — Dating Chris for a month
- **Chris (he/him)** — Maya’s boyfriend

### scene_1 — Maya

I am curled up with Chris on a cozy night in, a month into dating, when our kissing slows and he goes quiet. I remember the FRIES model: freely given, reversible, informed, enthusiastic, and specific. I cannot tell what his silence means, so I need to make room for an answer.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I pause and ask, "Would you like to keep kissing, take a break, or stop?"
   - **Feedback:** A specific check-in makes room for a freely given answer. Silence is a reason to pause, not permission to continue.
2. 1 point: I pause and wait for him to bring it up.
   - **Feedback:** Giving space is considerate. A gentle question also makes it clear that stopping is welcome.
3. 0 points: I keep kissing because he seemed interested a moment ago.
   - **Feedback:** Remembering earlier interest is understandable. Consent is current and specific, so pause when engagement changes.

### scene_2 — Maya

Chris says, "I guess we can keep going if you want," and I hear how much the answer centers me. I want him to feel wanted, not responsible for keeping the evening on track. I notice my urge to reassure him by asking again, but repeated requests could become pressure.

1. 0 points: I ask a few more times to help him feel certain.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting certainty is understandable. Repeated requests can wear someone down rather than create freely given consent.
2. **Best choice — 2 points**: I say, "You do not owe me a yes; we can stop without any disappointment to manage."
   - **Feedback:** Enthusiasm means genuine willingness, not a yes offered to protect someone else’s feelings.
3. 1 point: I ask once whether he is sure, without explaining that stopping is welcome.
   - **Feedback:** Seeking clarity makes sense. Naming that there is no obligation helps distinguish willingness from accommodation.

### scene_3 — Maya

We take a break, and Chris says he would enjoy sitting close but does not want more kissing tonight. I realize our expectations have been mostly unspoken since we started dating. I want to revisit our agreement without treating this new boundary as a problem he needs to solve.

1. 1 point: I agree to sit close tonight and leave future check-ins unmentioned.
   - **Feedback:** Respecting tonight’s limit matters. Revisiting agreements later also gives changing needs a clear place to be heard.
2. 0 points: I assume sitting close means kissing can restart if the mood improves.
   - **Feedback:** Hoping for closeness is understandable. Consent to one kind of contact does not extend to another.
3. **Best choice — 2 points**: I ask what sitting close means to him and suggest checking our boundaries again another day.
   - **Feedback:** Agreements are living understandings. Individual, specific check-ins matter in every relationship structure.

### scene_4 — Maya

Chris tells me he felt rushed before I noticed him going quiet, and my first impulse is to explain that I meant well. I feel embarrassed, but I do not want him to have to comfort me. I have a chance to listen, own my part, and ask what would help him feel respected now.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I thank him, apologize for rushing, and ask what he needs without defending my intentions.
   - **Feedback:** Accountability centers the person affected: listen, take responsibility, and make a concrete change.
2. 0 points: I explain my intentions until he understands I care.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting to be understood is human. Listening first keeps the impact on him from getting lost behind your intentions.
3. 1 point: I apologize quickly and suggest watching something else.
   - **Feedback:** An apology can open repair. Asking what he needs keeps the next step from becoming another assumption.

### scene_end — Maya

I let the evening be quiet without asking it to become anything else, and Chris tells me what would feel comfortable next time. Practicing ongoing consent and accountability does not make us perfect, but it gives our new relationship more room for honesty and trust.


## 2. Before the Third Date
**Module:** `safer-sex`
**Story slug:** `before-the-third-date`
**Difficulty:** 1 · **XP reward:** 50

### Characters
- **Dev (he/him)** — 45, dating again after divorce
- **Renee (she/her)** — Woman Dev is seeing

### scene_1 — Dev

At 45, I am dating again after a divorce, and my third date with Renee is tomorrow. I want to talk about protection before we get closer, but I worry that bringing it up will sound suspicious. I remind myself that harm reduction is shared care, not a judgment about either of us.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I ask for a calm time to discuss protection and what level of risk feels acceptable to each of us.
   - **Feedback:** Harm reduction supports informed choices and practical prevention without demanding zero risk or using shame.
2. 1 point: I buy barriers but leave the conversation for later.
   - **Feedback:** Preparing supplies is useful. Talking first lets both people shape prevention and boundaries.
3. 0 points: I decide that raising protection would make her think I distrust her.
   - **Feedback:** Protecting a new connection makes sense. A care-focused conversation can build trust rather than question it.

### scene_2 — Dev

Renee welcomes the conversation and asks when I last had STI testing. I realize I remember the appointment but not which tests were included or when they were appropriate. I want an honest answer that makes space for both of us to get useful guidance instead of pretending I know more.

1. 0 points: I say looking healthy means we probably do not need testing.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting reassurance is understandable. Appearance does not replace testing or an informed prevention conversation.
2. **Best choice — 2 points**: I share what I know and suggest asking a clinician which tests and timing fit our circumstances.
   - **Feedback:** Testing is routine care. A clinician can tailor tests and timing; an unspecified past test does not answer every question.
3. 1 point: I tell her the date of my last appointment but do not check what was tested.
   - **Feedback:** Sharing a date is a start. Knowing what was tested and getting timing guidance makes the information more useful.

### scene_3 — Dev

We discuss using barriers, and Renee says she does not want them treated as a measure of how serious we are. I feel relieved because I do not want affection to become a promise about protection. I need to help turn our general intention into an explicit agreement we can both revisit.

1. 1 point: I agree that we will use barriers but leave updates undefined.
   - **Feedback:** Agreeing on barriers helps. Discussing changes and check-ins makes the agreement easier to maintain.
2. 0 points: I suggest dropping barriers once the relationship feels serious.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting trust is understandable. Emotional closeness does not replace a separate, informed prevention decision.
3. **Best choice — 2 points**: I clarify barrier use, how we share updates, and when we will revisit the agreement.
   - **Feedback:** Prevention agreements work best when explicit, informed, and revisable. Changing barriers is not proof of commitment.

### scene_4 — Dev

As we plan our next steps, I admit I feel too old to ask basic sexual health questions. Renee says she also has questions she has never asked a provider. I want us to find care that respects our ages, bodies, and choices rather than let embarrassment decide what information we receive.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I look for an inclusive clinician and write down my questions before booking care.
   - **Feedback:** Sexual healthcare belongs to every age group. An affirming provider can offer relevant prevention guidance without assumptions.
2. 0 points: I stop asking because I think adults our age ought to know already.
   - **Feedback:** Feeling exposed is understandable. Learning has no age limit, and respectful professional support is available.
3. 1 point: I write questions down but wait indefinitely to contact a clinic.
   - **Feedback:** Writing questions helps organize care. Booking a visit turns preparation into access to professional guidance.

### scene_end — Dev

I book a sexual health visit and leave our next date free of any promise to become closer physically. Practicing harm reduction and safer-sex communication helps me feel less like I need every answer and more like we can make informed agreements together.


## 3. Different Speeds

I have just read about responsive desire, which sometimes grows from freely chosen closeness rather than arriving first.

   - **Feedback:** Self-understanding can ease shame. Sharing the pattern may also help Tom stop interpreting it as rejection.
3. 0 points: I promise to start every time he wants so my desire can catch up.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting reassurance for him makes sense. Responsive desire is not a reason to override your own willingness.

### scene_2 — Priya

Tonight I am mentally sorting school forms while Tom tries to make the room romantic. I like the attention, but my mind stays on tomorrow’s tasks and the pressure to respond. I recognize the Dual Control Model: adding accelerators may not help much while my brakes are still pressed.

1. 0 points: I try harder to feel interested so his effort will pay off.
   - **Feedback:** Caring about his effort is understandable. Turning desire into a duty can add pressure to the brakes.
2. **Best choice — 2 points**: I name stress and pressure as brakes and ask us to share tasks before considering closeness.
   - **Feedback:** The Dual Control Model includes accelerators and brakes. Reducing inhibition can matter more than adding stimulation.
3. 1 point: I ask for softer lighting but do not mention the unfinished tasks.
   - **Feedback:** A comfortable setting may help an accelerator. The active brakes of stress and obligation still deserve attention.

### scene_3 — Priya

Tom tells me he misses feeling wanted, and I miss feeling free to be affectionate without an expected next step. I hear two needs instead of one person being the problem. I want to discuss our desire discrepancy without making either of us the standard the other needs to match.

1. 1 point: I reassure him that I love him but avoid talking about affection.
   - **Feedback:** Reassurance matters. Concrete conversations about needs can help love feel more understandable in daily life.
2. 0 points: I tell him we need to agree on one correct frequency.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting predictability makes sense. A single target cannot replace ongoing consent and changing needs.
3. **Best choice — 2 points**: I ask what helps him feel wanted and share ways I enjoy affection without an expectation of sex.
   - **Feedback:** Desire discrepancy is common. Naming both people’s needs supports connection without turning affection into an obligation.

### scene_4 — Priya

We find a quiet evening in the calendar, and I notice myself treating it like an appointment to produce desire. I would rather make space to breathe, notice my body, and see what feels welcome. I also want real support if the distress that brought this up keeps getting in our way.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I plan pressure-free time and a body scan, and suggest a sex-positive therapist if distress continues.
   - **Feedback:** Cultivating desire means supporting context, not guaranteeing an outcome. Professional support can help with ongoing distress.
2. 0 points: I cancel all planned closeness until spontaneous desire returns.
   - **Feedback:** Avoiding pressure is understandable. Optional, pressure-free connection can be worthwhile without waiting for one desire pattern.
3. 1 point: I reserve time together but keep a private goal of feeling desire by the end.
   - **Feedback:** Making time is caring. Releasing the outcome makes that time an invitation rather than a performance test.

### scene_end — Priya

I put a quiet evening on our calendar as an invitation, not a requirement, and keep professional support on our list if we stay distressed. Practicing desire awareness helps me describe my responsive desire with less shame while leaving room for both Tom’s feelings and my choices.


## 4. New Body, Same Me
**Module:** `sexual-wellness-basics`
**Story slug:** `new-body-same-me`
**Difficulty:** 2 · **XP reward:** 50

### Characters
- **Carmen (she/her)** — 52, navigating a year of body changes
- **Luis (he/him)** — Carmen’s partner of ten years

### scene_1 — Carmen

At 52, after a year of body changes, I feel like a stranger in my own skin and keep avoiding closeness with Luis, my partner of ten years. I catch myself comparing my body to an old photograph. I want a starting point that respects the body I have instead of making intimacy a reward for changing it.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I tell Luis I need curiosity about what feels comfortable now, not comparisons with my past body.
   - **Feedback:** Bodies vary and change. Respectful, individual curiosity is more useful than treating one body or life stage as the standard.
2. 1 point: I put the photograph away but keep my worries to myself.
   - **Feedback:** Reducing comparison can help. Sharing your needs gives Luis a chance to support comfort without guessing.
3. 0 points: I decide closeness must wait until I look like the photograph again.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting familiarity makes sense. Your present body deserves respect and connection without meeting an appearance goal.

### scene_2 — Carmen

When I do choose some closeness, my interest builds and then fades while we are still together. I start watching myself from the outside, wondering whether I am moving through the right steps. I want to stop treating arousal like a staircase that must keep going upward to count.

1. 0 points: I push through so the evening follows the sequence I expect.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting an understandable pattern is human. Arousal does not owe anyone a sequence, and stopping remains an option.
2. **Best choice — 2 points**: I say we can slow down or stop, because arousal can rise and fall without needing a finish line.
   - **Feedback:** Nonlinear arousal can build and recede. Physical responses also do not determine consent or what you want.
3. 1 point: I take a short pause but quietly judge whether I am back on track.
   - **Feedback:** Pausing supports comfort. Letting go of a required sequence can reduce the self-monitoring that interrupts presence.

### scene_3 — Carmen

The next morning, I notice I am evaluating my reflection before I have even noticed how I feel. I sit down and place a hand on my arm, but my thoughts jump straight to appearance again. I want to practice embodiment without demanding that I suddenly love every part of myself.

1. 1 point: I repeat a kind phrase about my appearance without noticing sensation.
   - **Feedback:** Kind self-talk can support acceptance. Noticing physical sensations adds the embodied part of the practice.
2. 0 points: I study my reflection until I find every change I need to fix.
   - **Feedback:** Trying to regain control makes sense. More evaluation can deepen spectatoring; gentle sensory attention offers another path.
3. **Best choice — 2 points**: I notice warmth and pressure in my arm and gently return to sensation when judgment appears.
   - **Feedback:** Embodiment means experiencing your body from within. Body acceptance can begin with respect rather than constant admiration.

### scene_4 — Carmen

I want this shift to last longer than one encouraging morning, so I think about a small daily routine. The changes still worry me, and avoiding closeness has become a pattern I cannot easily loosen alone. I need care that includes both a manageable wellness practice and knowledgeable support.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I plan a brief morning body check-in and contact an affirming clinician about my changes and concerns.
   - **Feedback:** A wellness toolkit can combine small embodiment practices and professional care tailored to your body and concerns.
2. 0 points: I choose a demanding routine to make my body respond like it used to.
   - **Feedback:** Wanting improvement is understandable. Sustainable care supports your current body rather than requiring it to return to the past.
3. 1 point: I begin a body check-in but avoid mentioning persistent concerns to anyone.
   - **Feedback:** A small practice is useful. Ongoing concerns also deserve professional attention rather than becoming yours to manage alone.

### scene_end — Carmen

I arrange a healthcare visit and try a short body check-in before breakfast, without expecting instant ease. Practicing embodiment and body acceptance helps me approach Luis with a clearer sense of comfort, and I can keep learning what closeness means for me now.

Sam asks whether I want to try something different tonight, and I feel tempted to turn exploration into another assignment.    - **Feedback:** Wanting useful feedback makes sense. Sensate focus practices presence, not achieving a particular response.
3. **Best choice — 2 points**: I invite a brief, agreed hand-and-arm touch exercise focused on sensation, with no goal or escalation.

### scene_4 — Jordan

I need words that guide him kindly and a source of support if shame keeps closing my voice down.

1. **Best choice — 2 points**: I say, "I like when we slow down," and suggest a sex therapist if shame keeps blocking these conversations.

2. 0 points: I say nothing and hope he can figure out my preferences.

3. 1 point: I say I enjoyed the evening without naming what I liked.
   - **Feedback:** Appreciation matters. A specific preference gives Sam useful guidance and makes your own experience easier to share.

### scene_end — Jordan

I tell Sam one preference and ask about his, without expecting either conversation to be complete. Practicing pleasure awareness and pleasure communication gives us a kinder starting point, and I know professional support is available if shame continues to keep me quiet.
